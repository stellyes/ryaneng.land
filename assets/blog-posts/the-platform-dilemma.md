Okay... look. This isn't a personal attack

Unless of course, you do take offense before reading a word of this, in which case _this is written about you specifically._

I'm not in a position to say that social media is going to ruin your artistic integrity. I use social media both casually and for promotional reasons. I believe I am immune to this, because I have an undying urge to create like the bus from the 1994 movie "Speed" and I have an inability to stop. I possess creativity like it's a non-contagious virus. If I stop, I am simply dead. I hope heaven has a recording booth.

I notice a growing trend with people online becoming artists entirely for the sake of "blowing up." What a stupid phrase "blowing up" is. There's a palpable disingenuous feeling to anything created for clout. I believe the great artists before me called this:

<h1 style="text-align: center">"poser behavior"</h1>

\- and they couldn't be more right as far as I'm concerned. Interests and hobbies stem from desire, a curiosity that is either satisfied or stays with you until it manifests into purpose. If your purpose is to expose yourself to the world by any means necessary, start an OnlyFans.

I greatly fear for the new generation of "artists" and artists alike. Will the artist be washed away by the sewage that imitates them? Or will passion persevere?

I aim to (maybe) answer this question and dive a bit into what others before me have concluded about posers being posers. More academically said,

<em>"When exposure becomes the goal of creation, will the work shift from expressing the self to performing it?"</em>

<h1><span style="font-family: Warbler; font-size: 0.8em;">I. </span>Ego Indulgence</h1>

Platforms designed to maximize engagement naturally attract people who want their slice of that attention.

No shit. This is reinforced by a meta-analysis by Jessica McCain and W. Keith Campbell titled "Narcissism and social media use." Across 62 studies and 13,000 participants, there was found to be a reliable link between narcissistic and a range of social media behaviors, including the frequency in which people post as it relates to the number of followers they have. The authors posture that "individuals with pre-existing narcissism are drawn to social media."

I want to briefly sidebar - this seems a bit extreme to me, or maybe "Narcissism" has been used to too often in a diagnostic context for us to remember it can be used as an adjective to describe patterns, not dictate identity. That is to say, narcissistic behavior does not mean you have narcissistic personality disorder. Big distinction for the topic at hand.

A separate analysis touches on this more directly, "Narcissism and Social Networking Behavior," by authors T. Gnambs and M. Appel.

Between both analyses, it becomes clear how strong the link between these behaviors and narcissism really is. Personally, I don't think it's all too present. I believe these analyses touch on good subject matter, but stretch their findings to adhere to their thesis.

This becomes a case of "causation does not equal correlation," and for my own, selfish sake I'm going to have to agree. It's a pretty bold assumption to claim that platform use and its frequency is dictated by the users' egos entirely. By this logic, the authors who published these meta-analyses would be considered textbook narcissists, no? They did go through a publishing firm, after all.

---

<h1><span style="font-family: Warbler; font-size: 0.8em;">II. </span>The Chase and its Effect</h1>

_This is where my belief lies, where my issues manifest with the so-called "Platform Dilemma."_ I've ruled out the oversimplified idea that everyone on the internet is a narcissist, but there's clearly an effect that takes hold on the users of these social media platforms. Some people enter the creative space already swallowed by this mindset, while others are slowly consumed by it before the original goal becomes lost.

For this, I read the 30+ page meta-analysis, "[A review] of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation," by R.M. Ryan, R. Koestner, and E.L. Deci. In their review of 128 experiments, expected tangible rewards (the kind promised in advance for doing a task) reduced people's intrinsic motivation. In laymen's terms:

_"When your creative outlet is measured in likes, shares, or any kind of engagement, you lose the plot."_

When your creative outlet is rewarded and praised, there becomes this understanding that exposure equals success, and exposure can be measured in the quantifiable praise we've manufactured. The work stops being the point and becomes the vehicle. The inner child at play becomes an employee of the reward system. The authors use the term "the overjustification effect" to describe this phenomenon.

---

<h1><span style="font-family: Warbler; font-size: 0.8em;">III. </span>Being Yourself as a Profession</h1>

I hesitate to consider this a distinct finding against the previous section... but there is nuance here. The subtle dilemma with being a creative on these platforms is that it blurs the line between self-expression and labor.

Communication scholars Brooke Erin Duffy and Emily Wissinger analyzed more than 200 articles about creator careers. They found a recurring story about “the fun, free, and authentic nature of their self-starter careers.” Beneath it, they argue, sits a demand for constant self-branding and always-on entrepreneurial work.

Where do you start and end when your self-expression is equally the product?

---

<h1><span style="font-family: Warbler; font-size: 0.8em;">IV. </span>I'm Lying to You</h1>

What if none of this is true at all?

No, I believe there is a shred of truth in the above points. However, if I'm going to sit here and claim to be an academic, I need to also add the research I did that exists contrary to my beliefs.

In a 2020 survey of 377 YouTube and Twitch creators, Marko Törhönen and colleagues found nearly the opposite. Income and prestige were less significant drivers than enjoyment and socialisation, even as the activity grows more commercial.

I will say, when you ask the animal born in the zoo if they feel contained, the obvious response would be that they don't.

To elaborate further, the creators surveyed named intrinsic reasons as their main motivation, not the fame. They also listed the social aspect of being a notable social media figure. Wanting human connection with other people counts as intrinsic motive, yes, but I would argue that this study focuses more on people engaging in the online space strictly as a content creator, less about a creative that makes content. This ties directly in with the third point I made here. _Where do you start and end when your self-expression is equally the product?_

<h1><span style="font-family: Warbler; font-size: 0.8em;">V. </span>Drawing the Curtains Close</h1>

These platforms document lifetimes worth of content, uploaded every single day. There is no way to make discerning arguments, especially when the algorithms that feed us the content cater to our attention spans. I think it all really boils down to this:

_If you knew that you would never receive recognition for your artistic ventures, would you still do it?_

I stand on the claim that most modern creatives would not. The ones that would... well... probably wouldn't know that I asked this question.
